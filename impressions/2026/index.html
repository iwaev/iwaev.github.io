IAYC 2026

Mazarete, Spain • Chara (Cyprus)

When I came across an IAYC ad last December, I knew I was signing up for something awesome. Well, the camp was everything I expected and hoped for, but x100.

From my first interactions with super friendly and welcoming oldies before we even got to the camphouse, to leaders coming up to me and going “oh you’re the lawyer from Cyprus!” before I even got the chance to introduce myself to any of them yet, the atmosphere already felt like a warm hug from the first moment. Apart from the surprise element in different games and silly camp traditions, I felt like I fit in already from day one.

As a non-STEM major, I was a bit worried that I couldn’t live up to the project expectations. My impostor syndrome didn’t last for longer than the first Working Group session though, as our leader, Zooey, reassured me, and soon enough I realised that my working group partners were equally as inexperienced in telescope observations as I was. The approach of the leaders is to give you guidance and resources for your project, but to let you learn the ropes on your own, which allowed us to experiment a lot before finding what worked best for our project’s needs. Trying to take pictures of Saturn’s moons with a telescope while having little to no prior skill in practical astronomy and astrophotography was a humbling experience but definitely a very rewarding one as well, and sharing it with my working group partners was a big part of that. In IAYC you learn so many things that go beyond university lectures, and you get to do so by trial and error and a lot of guidance from experienced leaders.

You also get to learn a lot about other people’s passions and niches and share your own with them! People at the IAYC are very passionate about space and intricate topics, and thus were very excited to learn about my niche (space law), which is a topic often overlooked by other law students and lawyers. There is no shortage of things you can learn here about different scientific branches, different cultures, and perspectives in general, be it from workshops, late-night talks, or memorable conversations with people. A lot of these will stay with me for a long time.

One of my favourite memories from this year’s camp was the total solar eclipse. It was the most beautiful thing I have seen in my entire life, and sharing it with everyone at the camp made it a very emotional experience. The same night, it was the peak of the perseid meteor shower. We gathered with our red torches in the observation field under the dark sky, singing, playing guitars, and watching the massive perseids, one after another. I remember myself lying on the ground, stargazing, everyone around me singing, and feeling a sense of wholeness and totality (like the eclipse that day); a sense that in that moment I did not need anything else to be happy.

If I start listing all of the moments that shifted something in me at this camp, this text will become a book. I just want to say that I am eternally grateful to everyone at the camp who made my first experience so beautiful, and to all the people who tirelessly worked behind the scenes to make it possible, as well as the grant donors who gave me the opportunity to attend the camp.

I read somewhere on the website when I was applying, that time at camp works differently and that it goes both too slow and too fast at the same time. I didn’t know what that meant at the time, but now that I lived through it, I completely understand, and there is no better way to explain this weird time warping effect. It’s like being in a parallel dimension for a while and then being pulled out of it. It’s interesting how I can only think of sci-fi concepts to describe this dream-like state; maybe this is also a camp effect.

I can’t wait to join our weird little astronomy bubble next year. I am still in disbelief that this is a real experience and that it really happened to me. Ad astra, my friends!
